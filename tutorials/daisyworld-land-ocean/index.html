Daisyworld, land and ocean

A planet of land and ocean. Black and white daisies grow only on the land, so they can change the colour of the land but not of the sea. The ocean is dark, and it stores far more heat than land. That changes how far the daisies can steer the climate, and how fast the planet responds when the sun changes. A bare planet with the same land and ocean runs alongside for comparison.

The planet

Things to try

  1. All land, slow brightening. Compare the daisy planet with the bare planet. Over what range of sun does the daisy planet stay near the daisies' favourite temperature?
  2. Add ocean. Switch to 30% ocean, then 70%. What happens to that range: does it get wider, narrower, or move? At 70% ocean, are the daisies still in control? Tap the chart where they are most common and compare the two planets.
  3. Two reasons. More ocean means less land for the daisies, and a darker planet. Set the ocean albedo to 0.5, the same as bare ground. Which change came from the darker ocean, and which from less land?
  4. Sudden change, all land. How long does the bare planet take to warm? What does the daisy planet do in the first months, and over the next ten years? Press "Keep this run to compare" above the charts.
  5. Sudden change, 30% ocean. Compare with the kept run. Turn on the log time axis. How much slower is the warming now, and why? Read the response time under the planet settings.
  6. A deeper ocean. Keep the 30% ocean run, then set the mixed layer to 200 m. Is the daisy planet's peak warming bigger or smaller? Who gets more time to react?
  7. Sudden change, 70% ocean. How long does the bare planet take to warm? And the daisy planet? When is its warming over?
  8. Too fast to follow. Brighten, then dim, with a 30-year run and a 300 m mixed layer. Does the planet keep up with the sun? Compare with a 600-year run.

How the model works

The whole planet is one box with one temperature T. Daisies cover fractions aw (white) and ab (black) of the land; the rest of the land, x, is bare. The ocean, a fraction 1 − f of the planet, has a fixed albedo.

albedoA = f·(a_w A_w + a_b A_b + x A_g) + (1−f)·A_o
energyC dT/dt = S·L·(1−A) − σT⁴
storageC = f·C_land + (1−f)·ρc·h
patchesT_w = T + q′(A − A_w), T_b = T + q′(A − A_b)
growthβ(T) = 1 − 0.003265 (T_opt − T)² (zero outside ±17.5 °C)
daisiesda_i/dt = a_i [ x β(T_i) − γ ]

The heat capacity C decides how fast the planet responds. If the sun changes suddenly, the temperature moves most of the way to its new value in about τ = C / (4σT³). Land plus air has C_land = 10⁷ J m⁻² K⁻¹, so τ is about 3 weeks. A 50 m ocean mixed layer stores about 20 times as much, so τ is many months. The daisies take years to spread or die back.

f = land fraction; h = mixed-layer depth; ρc = 4.1 × 10⁶ J m⁻³ K⁻¹ (sea water); S = 917 W m⁻²; time in years. The response time shown under the planet settings uses T = 15 °C. Daisy cover never falls below 1% of land (seeds). In this one-box model the land feels the same temperature as the ocean. On Earth the land warms faster than the ocean, but each is held back by the other through the air.

Sources

Watson, A. J. and Lovelock, J. E. (1983). Biological homeostasis of the global environment: the parable of Daisyworld. Tellus 35B, 284–289. The growth curve, albedos, S, γ = 0.3 and q′ = 20 K follow this paper.
