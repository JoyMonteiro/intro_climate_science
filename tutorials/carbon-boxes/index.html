The model

This is the "BEAM" model of Glotter et al. (2014), which goes back to Bolin and Eriksson (1959). Three boxes hold carbon, in gigatonnes (GtC): the air (MAT), the top of the ocean (MUP, the top 1/51 of the ocean's volume, about 75 m) and the deep ocean (MLO, the other 50/51).

dM_AT/dt = E(t) − k_a (M_AT − A·B·M_UP) dM_UP/dt = k_a (M_AT − A·B·M_UP) − k_d (M_UP − M_LO/δ) dM_LO/dt = k_d (M_UP − M_LO/δ)

Each exchange wears away a difference: carbon moves until the two boxes it joins are in balance. MUP − MLO/δ is zero when the two parts of the ocean have the same carbon per litre (δ = 50 is the deep ocean's volume over the top's). The deep ocean's turnover time, the time to replace its water by exchange with the top, is δ/kd: 1,000 years with the paper's kd = 0.05 a year.

The chemistry. A·B is how much carbon the air holds, in balance, for each gigatonne in the top of the ocean. A is Henry's law: CO₂ dissolves in proportion to how much is in the air. B is the catch. Only a small part of the ocean's carbon is dissolved CO₂ gas; the rest is bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and carbonate (CO₃²⁻) ions:

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ 1/B = 1 + k₁/[H⁺] + k₁k₂/[H⁺]² (all the carbon, per unit of dissolved CO₂)

Before we started, 1/B was about 170 in this model: for every CO₂ molecule dissolved as gas, the top of the ocean held about 170 carbon atoms as ions. That is why the ocean holds so much more carbon than the air (about 60 times as much, in this model). But adding CO₂ makes seawater more acidic (more H⁺), which pushes the reactions to the left, so 1/B falls (to about 120 by 2024 in the "Faster deep exchange" run): the more carbon the ocean has taken, the less ready it is to take more. This is the Revelle (buffer) factor. The model finds [H⁺] at each step from the upper ocean's carbon, holding its alkalinity (the seawater's capacity to neutralise acid) fixed. Turn the chemistry off and B stays at its pre-industrial value.

The emissions

"Our emissions" are, for each year from 1750 to 2024, the carbon that ended up in the air or the sea: the atmospheric growth plus the ocean sink in the Global Carbon Budget 2025 (Friedlingstein et al. 2025, historical budget). That is our fossil and land-use emissions minus what the land took up. The model has no land, so this is the part it has to deal with: about GtC in all. After 2024 the app uses the 2015–2024 average, GtC a year, until emissions stop. Observed CO₂ is the annual mean at Mauna Loa (NOAA GML); the observed ocean sink is from the Global Carbon Budget.

Testing it

  • Today's ocean sink. With the paper's numbers the ocean takes up too little today: about 1.3 GtC a year in 2014–2024 against the Global Carbon Budget's 3.2, so the air ends up with too much CO₂ (480 ppm in 2024). Shorten the deep-ocean turnover to 200 years (kd = 0.25 a year) and the model follows Mauna Loa (313, 359 and 422 ppm in 1959, 1994 and 2024, against 316, 359 and 425 measured) and takes up 3.3 GtC a year in 2014–2024 (preset "Faster deep exchange"). Neither is right: one deep box has to stand for both the thermocline, which takes up carbon within decades, and the abyss, which takes a thousand years. Where the carbon finally settles doesn't depend on these exchange times at all, only on the chemistry and the size of the ocean.
  • Where it settles. In ocean-only models compared by Archer et al. (2009), about 20% of a 1,000 GtC pulse stays in the air once the ocean has caught up, and about 35% of a 5,000 GtC pulse. This model gives % and %.
  • How fast. Archer et al. (2009): the ocean takes up most of the carbon over 2 to 20 centuries. Joos et al. (2013), comparing many models with land and ocean: of 100 GtC added to today's air, 25 ± 9% is still there after 1,000 years.

What it leaves out

  • Land. Plants and soils take up about a fifth of today's emissions (21% over 2015–2024, Global Carbon Budget 2025).
  • The sea floor and rocks. Over thousands of years, the more acidic water dissolves calcium carbonate on the sea floor, which restores the buffer and draws the air down further (3,000–7,000 years, Archer et al. 2009). Over hundreds of thousands of years, the weathering of rocks takes the rest. So the level where this model settles is not the end: it is where the ocean alone would leave us.
  • Warming. A warmer ocean holds less CO₂, so the real air keeps somewhat more. Glotter et al. include this; here it is left out.
  • Biology and ocean currents, except as the two exchange times.
